The Story
Read carefully. Notice the bold words.
🏆

The ancient Greeks loved stories about brave, strong heroes like Odysseus. But they also found another way to celebrate courage and strength — a massive festival called the Olympic Games. The bravest, most powerful Greeks from every city traveled to compete for glory and prizes.

The Olympics began after two rival Greek cities finally made peace, following years of fighting one another. To celebrate this hard-won peace, the two cities decided to hold a great festival in honor of Zeus, the chief god of all the Greek gods. They named the festival after Mount Olympus, the tallest mountain in Greece, where the Greeks believed Zeus and the other gods lived.

At this first festival, the Greeks feasted together and offered sacrifices to Zeus. They also held races, and the winners received wreaths made of olive branches to wear proudly on their heads — the olive leaves were a symbol of peace between the once-warring cities.

The Greeks decided the Olympics should happen every four years, always in honor of Zeus. As the years passed, more and more Greeks from different cities began traveling to compete, and organizers kept adding new kinds of events. Soon athletes were racing horses as well as running on foot. Boxing and wrestling matches were added, along with a demanding new event called the pentathlon, in which one athlete had to win five different competitions: throwing the discus, throwing the javelin, long-jumping, wrestling, and running.

However, only men were allowed to compete in the ancient Olympics. Girls could watch from the sidelines, but married women were completely forbidden from attending, on pain of death — the Greeks believed that only men could demonstrate true bravery and strength, and that training a strong, graceful male body was the finest way to honor the gods.

For nearly a thousand years, the Olympics continued every four years. Crowds traveled from across Greece, camping together, feasting each evening, and listening to poets recite thrilling stories — perhaps even the tale of Odysseus and the Cyclops. Winners returned home as celebrated heroes, rewarded with banquets, money, and free food for the rest of their lives.

Today, the Olympic Games are still held every four years, now featuring hundreds of events — including running, wrestling, and boxing, just as in ancient times, alongside modern additions like swimming, gymnastics, and basketball. Both men and women compete, and athletes travel from every corner of the world, not just from Greece. Yet the Games still carry the name of Mount Olympus, and they still celebrate the very same qualities the ancient Greeks admired: strength, grace, and courage.

Language Focus
Grammar from the story — learn through meaning
📌 Passive voice for traditions and rules
Passive voice is common when describing rules or traditions, since the focus is on what was permitted or banned.
Only men were allowed to compete in the ancient Olympics.
Married women were forbidden from attending the Games.
New events were added as the Olympics grew larger.
💡 Passive voice ('was/were + past participle') shifts focus away from who made the rule.
📌 'Both...and...' / contrasting then and now
Use 'both...and' to show two things are true together, and words like 'then'/'now' to compare past and present.
Both men and women compete in the modern Olympics.
The ancient Games celebrated strength, grace, and courage — and the modern Games celebrate the very same qualities.
Then, only Greeks competed; now, athletes travel from every corner of the world.
💡 These structures are useful for comparing traditions across time.
📌 Superlatives in description
Superlatives (the + adjective + -est, or the most + adjective) describe the highest degree of a quality.
The bravest, most powerful Greeks traveled to compete.
Mount Olympus was the tallest mountain in Greece.
Training a strong body was considered the finest way to honor the gods.
💡 Superlatives are often used when describing legendary places, people, or achievements.

The ancient Olympics began as a celebration of peace between former enemies. Do you think sports can still help resolve conflicts between nations or groups today?
🚺
Women were completely forbidden from the ancient Olympics, yet today they compete equally with men. What does this change tell us about how societies' ideas of fairness can evolve over time?
🫒

🌍

📖 Chapter 20 Summary

In Chapter 20, we watched Greek civilization rebuild itself from the ashes of the Dark Ages. Wandering barbarian tribes — the Dorians and the Sea People — gradually settled down, built villages and cities, and became civilized Greeks, borrowing an alphabet from the Phoenicians so they could finally write down their myths and history. One of the first great Greek writers was Homer, a poet believed to have been blind, who recorded the story of the Trojan War in the Iliad and the adventures of the warrior Odysseus in the Odyssey. In one unforgettable tale, Odysseus and his men were trapped in the cave of a monstrous Cyclops, who devoured several of the sailors before Odysseus outwitted him with wine, a false name — 'Noman' — and a sharpened stake driven into the giant's single eye. The men escaped by clinging beneath the bellies of sheep, but Odysseus's pride led him to boast and reveal his true name, drawing a terrible curse from the Cyclops's father, Poseidon, god of the sea — a curse that turned Odysseus's short voyage home into a ten-year ordeal. Meanwhile, the Greeks found another way to celebrate courage and strength: the Olympic Games, born from peace between rival cities and held every four years in honor of Zeus. Though only men could compete in ancient times, the Games have grown over thousands of years into a modern celebration where athletes of every nation and gender compete for the same olive-wreath ideals of strength, grace, and courage.

💡 The Big Idea of Chapter 20

Civilizations can rebuild themselves from nothing — through writing, storytelling, cleverness, and shared celebration — and even the cleverest heroes must be careful, because pride can turn a short journey into a decade-long trial.
